# Workload Definition — Operational Purchasing BEP

**Status:** Canonical project definition, synchronized 24 August 2026.

**Ownership:** This file defines what **workload** means in the BEP. Measurement procedure belongs in `Phase_1_Current_Methodology.md`; literature status belongs in `literature/README.md`.

---

# 1. Governing source

**Young, M. S., Brookhuis, K. A., Wickens, C. D., & Hancock, P. A. (2015).** *State of science: Mental workload in ergonomics*. *Ergonomics, 58*(1), 1–17. https://doi.org/10.1080/00140139.2014.956151

Young et al. treat mental workload as a **multidimensional construct** shaped by the interaction of:

- **task characteristics**, including demands and required performance;
- **operator characteristics**, including skill, experience and attention;
- **environmental context/support**, including external and technological support and competing demands.

Their global framing centres on the attentional resources required to meet objective and subjective performance criteria. This is the **governing workload framework for the BEP**.

---

# 2. Project interpretation

For this project, mental workload means the attentional/cognitive resources the operational buyer must mobilize to perform purchasing work to the required standard under the actual task, operator and environmental conditions.

Examples of relevant factors are:

| Young et al. perspective | Purchasing examples |
|---|---|
| **Task** | complexity, information volume, uncertainty, exceptions, verification requirement, concurrent demands |
| **Operator** | experience, tacit knowledge, familiarity, attention, automatic versus controlled processing |
| **Environment / support** | interruptions, colleague support, Exact/Orbis support, task switching, information availability |
| **Required performance** | correct quantity/price/allocation, correct PO, timely ordering, no duplicate demand, correct confirmation handling |

A task can therefore be short but cognitively demanding, or long and repetitive with relatively low cognitive demand.

---

# 3. Operational effort is related but not identical

The project also measures process burden using indicators such as:

- task frequency;
- active processing time;
- elapsed time;
- line/document volume;
- manual actions;
- rework;
- hand-offs and interruptions.

These measures help quantify **operational effort**, but they do not by themselves define mental workload.

A prioritization estimate such as:

`operational effort volume = frequency × representative active processing time`

may be used for comparable task categories. It must **not** be reported as the complete workload definition or as a complete mental-workload score.

---

# 4. Reporting rule

When the BEP uses the word **workload** without qualification, interpret it through the Young et al. framework.

Use more specific terms when only one dimension is measured, for example:

- `active processing time`;
- `elapsed time`;
- `operational effort volume`;
- `verification volume`;
- `rework burden`;
- `judgement / attentional demand`.

Unless a validated combined instrument is introduced later, heterogeneous workload indicators should not be arbitrarily merged into one numerical workload score.

The current observation variables and evaluation approach are defined in `Phase_1_Current_Methodology.md`.
